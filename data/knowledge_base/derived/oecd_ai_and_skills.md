## Key Findings

OECD work on AI and skills focuses on how AI changes the skills people need at
work and how education, training, and adult learning systems can respond.

AI does not only create demand for technical specialists. It also changes the
tasks of non-specialists who need to work with AI systems, assess outputs, and
adapt workflows.

Training systems need to keep pace with changing task requirements. For
individual learners, this makes practical evidence of skill more valuable than
passive familiarity with AI terminology.

## Implications for AI Learners

Learners should build skills that help them use AI reliably: understanding model
limits, evaluating outputs, protecting data, and applying AI to real tasks.

RAG evaluation, source grounding, and AI output testing are useful because they
make AI work inspectable instead of purely experimental.

## Implications for Developers Transitioning into AI Engineering

Developers should connect AI skills to engineering practices: test design,
observability, deployment, security, and maintenance.

The transition into AI Engineering is stronger when the learner can show how AI
features are evaluated and improved, not only how they are prompted.

## Learning and Career Decision Guidance

When comparing RAG evaluation, LangGraph, Docker, and AI agents, OECD-style
skills evidence supports prioritizing skills that improve reliable AI use and
training transfer. RAG evaluation is often a strong first choice because it
supports quality control, source grounding, and practical AI system judgment.
